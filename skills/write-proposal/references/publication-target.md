# Shape a proposal for its publication target

Use when a target conference, journal, track, or article type matters to the requested research plan. Plan the research and its argument; do not expand the task into paper submission, autonomous experiments, or reviewer correspondence.

## Establish the target and the research purpose

Identify the intended audience, research question, contribution type, and publication target already chosen by the researcher. Contribution types may include a method, explanation, theory, measurement, replication, dataset, system, or synthesis; do not assume every venue wants a new algorithm.

Record the exact venue, conference edition or year, track, and submission type; for a journal, record article type and any special issue. A main track, workshop, short paper, and journal research article can have different purposes and rules despite related names.

If no venue is chosen, proceed with a contribution-centered proposal. Recommend a small shortlist only when requested, based on checked scope, evidence needs, and resource fit. Make a delegated recommendation with reasons; otherwise preserve the user's selection. Do not block writing on an unsolicited venue interview or silently replace a chosen venue.

## Build a small, sourced target profile

Check current official calls, aims and scope, author instructions, and review criteria with available search tools. Read the exact year, track, and article type, and record URLs, relevant sections, and access dates. Do not present remembered rules, old calls, or search snippets as current requirements. Supplied excerpts can support a bounded profile without implying live verification. If current rules are unavailable, mark them unverified and continue the scientific plan; do not invent a deadline, page count, reviewer preference, or acceptance likelihood.

Only include constraints that change this proposal or its feasibility:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Identity and purpose | Venue, edition, track/article type, intended readers, relevant scope. |
| Contribution and evaluation | Official criteria; the type of claim and evidence this proposal must support. |
| Format and timing | Relevant submission length, what counts toward it, deadlines with time zone and stage, or rolling submission when confirmed. A journal has no fixed annual deadline unless the source says so. |
| Research dependencies | Applicable artifact, reproducibility, data, or other stated requirements that affect what must be planned. Preserve whether each is mandatory, recommended, or optional. |
| Evidence status | Source/section/date, unverified or conflicting items, and the consequence for the plan. |

Separate **official requirements**, **observations from a small set of relevant published papers**, **your strategic recommendations**, and **the researcher's own constraints**. Published examples help identify related conversations and possible evidence styles, but do not prove an unwritten acceptance rule. A blog's “three benchmarks are required” is not a venue rule. Do not pad citations to appear aligned.

When sources conflict, check their authority, year, track, scope, and effective dates; do not mechanically prefer a newer page about a different track. State an unresolved conflict rather than claiming compliance. A prior edition can be labeled as planning context, never silently substituted for the chosen edition.

## Translate the profile into the proposal

For each consequential criterion, connect:

**Target criterion or audience need → candidate contribution → closest and contrary work → evidence or proof needed → resource dependency → proposal section and next decision.**

- Explain why this audience should care about the question. Keep the claim faithful to the evidence; changing the title or adding venue keywords is not a research strategy.
- Extend the literature survey to the relevant scientific conversation, including work outside the target venue. Identify closest alternatives and useful negative findings. Use a few inspected examples when they inform an actual design choice, not as a popularity ranking.
- Design the smallest adequate evidence for the claim: for example a proof, controlled comparison, replication, realistic workload, user study, or synthesis. Select based on the claim and checked criteria, not stereotypes such as “all conferences need novelty” or “all journals need more datasets.”
- Connect evaluation breadth, artifacts, analysis, and writing to the available resources. Keep estimated durations conditional. Do not promise that more compute, a long paper, or a large experiment count earns acceptance.
- Preserve informative negative outcomes. If the chosen track requires a contribution the evidence cannot support, expose that mismatch and recommend a concrete evidence, scope, or target change. Do not fabricate a new method or hide contrary results to fit the venue.
- For different targets, distinguish changes to emphasis and document structure from changes to the scientific question, study population, experiments, or claims. Explain material changes and preserve the researcher's choices; alternatives are recommendations until adopted.

A research proposal is not automatically a camera-ready paper. Apply submission length, anonymity, or template rules to the requested artifact only when relevant. Translate the eventual paper's constraints into research planning without forcing a short internal proposal into the full submission template. A venue's presentation poster does not automatically set the format of a requested proposal poster.

## Deliver and assess fit

Deliver the actual proposal or requested revision, with a compact target profile, criterion-to-evidence mapping, and consequential unresolved inputs. For a comparison, make the differences and added work explicit, then give a recommendation within the user's delegated scope. For an unknown target, keep those fields provisional instead of inventing a venue.

Report venue fit separately from scientific readiness: **supported within the inspected scope**, **conditional on named evidence**, **mismatch**, or **unassessed**. Explain the judgment and its source. A paper can be a useful study but a poor fit for one track; a suitable topic can still have a defective evaluation plan. Neither fit nor a proposal score predicts acceptance.

Preserve the target and evidence context with future revisions so that a change of audience is not reported as an improvement in research quality. Stop when the proposal expresses a credible route to the intended contribution and identifies the remaining scientific and target-specific gaps.
