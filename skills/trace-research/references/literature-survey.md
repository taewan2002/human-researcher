# Literature survey for a research decision

Use for a requested literature survey or the evidence base of a proposal. Follow the researcher's question and constraints; do not turn every paper lookup into a survey.

## Establish coverage

- Define the question, inclusion boundaries, relevant terminology, and search cutoff. Separate the date of the search from publication dates and the versions inspected.
- Combine foundations, recent developments, closest alternatives, and contrary or null evidence. Explain omissions that could change the judgment. Recency and citation count are search signals, not measures of quality.
- Search by the question and alternative formulations; follow verified citing papers and relevant author or lab trajectories. Use earlier references where needed. Dedupe versions without discarding material changes.
- Record locations searched, queries actually run, selection reasons, and access limits. Label a supplied-source survey as such; dates on supplied documents do not establish current field-wide coverage. Never turn a planned search into a completed one.
- Call the result a scoped narrative survey unless the work actually supports a systematic-review claim. Do not invent screening counts, exhaustive coverage, or a universal minimum paper count.
- If a publication target matters, inspect relevant work for its scientific conversation and evidence style while retaining closest or contrary work outside that venue. Record the edition, track, or journal article type. Patterns in selected published papers are observations, not official requirements or evidence of acceptance likelihood; do not inflate citations to signal venue fit.

## Synthesize, rather than stack summaries

Organize the evidence by approaches, assumptions, findings, or disagreements relevant to the question. A compact evidence table can contain:

| Source and inspected location | Question / approach | Conditions and reported evidence | Limitation or counterevidence | Consequence for this proposal |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep reported findings, your interpretation, and open hypotheses distinct. Do not pool incompatible settings or infer improvement from a citation. An abstract can identify a candidate; it cannot support details absent from the abstract.

Then explain:

1. **What is established within this source set?** State the conditions under which sources agree.
2. **Where do they disagree or leave uncertainty?** Check differences in assumptions, inputs, evaluation, access, and definitions before describing a scientific conflict.
3. **Which work is closest to the proposed idea?** Compare the actual change and the strongest simpler alternative, including work that weakens the novelty claim.
4. **Why investigate this now?** Identify a supported unresolved need, changed constraint, new capability, or newly testable question. An older question can be timely; popularity alone is not a reason.
5. **What changes in the proposal?** Connect the evidence to a bounded question, baseline, method choice, measurement, resource assumption, or reason to stop or redirect. A missing taxonomy branch is not itself a research gap.

Include a taxonomy when requested or useful for explaining approach families. Its classification edges are distinct from verified citation edges; preserve hybrid membership and unknown properties. A small survey need not produce a diagram.

## Deliver a usable evidence base

Return the scope and cutoff, synthesis with source locations, closest-work comparison, consequential coverage gaps, and a bounded next reading or search step. Preserve the user's chosen direction while explaining any evidence that challenges it.

For a later proposal assessment, carry forward the evidence behind timeliness, practical or scientific value, reasoning, feasibility, and evaluation. Do not assign proposal scores without a proposal or imply that an extensive bibliography establishes researcher understanding.

Stop when the survey supports the scoped research decision or when a consequential missing source requires access. State what remains unknown instead of promising exhaustive discovery.
